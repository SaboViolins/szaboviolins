Bridging Two Alchemies

Every few years, a headline appears somewhere — a magazine, a journal, a science blog — announcing that someone has finally discovered the secret of Stradivari. A magical combination of traditional medicine. A chemical additive found under a microscope. Sometimes the underlying research is serious — wood biodegradation and enzyme treatments, for instance, have a real scientific basis and deserve to be taken seriously, whatever one concludes from them. But the way these findings get repackaged into headlines is another matter: "the secret," singular, finally cracked. The story gets shared, discussed, and within a few months, forgotten. Then, a few years later, it happens again.

I don't believe there is a secret to discover. I think there is a body of knowledge that was once common practice — built up, generation after generation, through observation and repetition, by makers working with the materials available to them in a particular place and time. And then, over time, much of it faded — not because it was hidden, but because it was never written down. It didn't need to be. It was simply how things were done. It was lost as the lifestyle, the market and the needs of people have evolved and "modernized".

What I want to do in this post — and in the posts that will follow it — is not to announce a discovery. It's to build a bridge. On one side is what we know of the materials, tools, and empirical knowledge available to the Cremonese masters — Stradivari, Guarneri del Gesù, and their contemporaries — during the golden period. On the other side is what modern chemistry, physics, and optics can tell us today. Some of what they did, we can now explain. Some of what we do today, they could not have known, but arrived at anyway, through patient observation.

I won't be laying out a step-by-step recipe here. That was never the point, and honestly, over thirty years of work doesn't compress into a blog post. What I hope instead is that some of what follows makes you curious enough to look closer at your own instruments, your own materials, and start asking your own questions….
